# 09 – Evaluating Expert Advice & Transferability

> ## Why this document exists
>
> One of Alex's requirements for this project was identifying who I would **not** recommend following and explaining why.
>
> My first reaction was to interpret this as a request to identify the "weakest" practitioner in my research.
>
> After completing the research, I no longer think that is the right way to evaluate expert advice.
>
> Every practitioner included in this repository contributed ideas that influenced at least one decision documented throughout Documents 01–08.
>
> I therefore do not believe any of them should simply be labelled as someone others should "not follow."
>
> Instead, I reached a different conclusion.
>
> The more useful question became:
>
> **"Whose recommendations would I be least likely to apply unchanged when building the operating model documented in this repository?"**
>
> That distinction is important.
>
> Throughout this repository I consistently separated:
>
> - evidence from interpretation,
> - recommendations from decisions,
> - and expertise from transferability.
>
> I have applied the same approach here.
>
> This document is therefore not an evaluation of who is the better marketer.
>
> It is an evaluation of how directly each practitioner's published advice transfers to the specific LinkedIn Organic Content operating model developed in this repository.

---

# My Evaluation Criteria

Rather than comparing popularity or personal preference, I evaluated each practitioner using four questions.

| Evaluation Question | Why It Matters |
|---------------------|----------------|
| How transferable is the recommendation? | Advice that depends heavily on one environment often requires adaptation elsewhere. |
| What assumptions does the recommendation make? | Understanding assumptions helps determine whether advice fits a different organisation. |
| How much did this practitioner's work influence my operating model? | Some practitioners shaped core strategic decisions, while others strengthened specific execution areas. |
| Would I apply this advice without modification? | This became my practical test for determining where additional judgement was required. |

These criteria remained consistent with the research methodology documented in Document 01 and the decision framework applied throughout the repository.

---

# My Conclusion Before Evaluating Individual Practitioners

One of the biggest changes in my thinking during this project was realising that I no longer ask:

> **"Is this expert right?"**

Instead, I ask:

> **"What assumptions need to be true before this recommendation becomes useful?"**

That shift changed how I interpreted every source included in this repository.

For that reason, I do not think it is intellectually honest to recommend that people stop following any of the practitioners I researched.

What I can do—and what I believe demonstrates better judgement—is explain whose recommendations I would apply more cautiously when building the operating model documented in this repository.

# Practitioner I Would Be Most Cautious About Applying Directly — Adam Robinson

## Why I Chose Adam Robinson

This was the most difficult conclusion in the repository because I found Adam Robinson's work both practical and commercially valuable.

Several ideas from his newsletters influenced how I thought about execution, operational discipline and measurable marketing systems.

However, after comparing his work with the other practitioners included in this repository, I found that his published recommendations were generally less transferable to the specific problem I was trying to solve.

This conclusion should not be interpreted as a criticism of his expertise.

It reflects the scope of this repository.

---

## What I Learned From His Work

Adam Robinson consistently emphasises:

- operational discipline,
- repeatable GTM systems,
- measurable commercial outcomes,
- structured execution,
- and marketing accountability.

Those ideas strengthened the execution layer of my playbook, particularly Documents 04 and 05.

I consider those contributions valuable.

---

## Why I Would Be Cautious

The reason I would be cautious is not because I disagree with his recommendations.

It is because many of them appear to assume organisational conditions such as:

- established GTM processes,
- close sales and marketing alignment,
- mature operational reporting,
- sufficient organisational resources,
- and disciplined execution across multiple functions.

Those assumptions are reasonable.

However, they were not assumptions I made while designing this operating model.

My objective was to create a framework that remained applicable to a broader range of B2B SaaS teams, including organisations with fewer resources or less mature marketing operations.

For that reason, I concluded that Adam Robinson's recommendations often require adaptation before implementation rather than direct adoption.

## Would I Recommend Following Adam Robinson?

Yes—but not without adaptation.

If someone asked me for one practitioner whose recommendations I would be most cautious about applying directly to the operating model developed in this repository, my answer would be Adam Robinson.

That answer surprised me because I genuinely respect the quality of his work.

It also challenged one of my assumptions going into this project.

I initially expected that good marketing advice would naturally transfer across different organisations.

After comparing the practitioners included in this repository, I no longer believe that is always true.

Advice can be excellent while still depending on organisational conditions that are not universally available.

That is how I interpreted Adam Robinson's work.

---

## Why I Reached This Conclusion

The difference was not the quality of the recommendations.

The difference was the assumptions behind them.

Across the material I reviewed, many of Adam Robinson's recommendations appeared to assume:

- a mature GTM organisation,
- established sales and marketing alignment,
- reliable operational reporting,
- clearly defined commercial ownership,
- and the capacity to execute structured growth systems consistently.

Those assumptions make sense for the environments he typically writes about.

However, they were not assumptions I wanted this operating model to depend on.

Throughout Documents 01–08, I deliberately tried to build a framework that could still be useful for smaller B2B SaaS teams, earlier-stage organisations and marketing teams with fewer operational resources.

For that reason, I found myself adapting Adam Robinson's recommendations more frequently than those of several other practitioners.

---

## What I Would Do Instead

If I were working inside an organisation with a mature GTM function, I would apply much more of Adam Robinson's thinking directly.

However, for the operating model developed in this repository, I preferred recommendations that relied more heavily on:

- customer understanding,
- positioning,
- educational value,
- editorial judgement,
- and strategic clarity.

Those principles appeared repeatedly across several practitioners and required fewer organisational assumptions before becoming useful.

That is why practitioners such as Dave Gerhardt, Emily Kramer and Ross Simmonds influenced the strategic direction of this repository more heavily, while Adam Robinson influenced the execution layer.

# Practitioner I Would Also Apply Carefully — Elena Verna

## Why I Chose Elena Verna

Elena Verna's work was another example where I found myself separating the quality of the advice from its transferability.

I found her thinking particularly valuable when researching AI-enabled marketing workflows and experimentation.

However, many examples in her published work assume a level of AI adoption and operational maturity that many organisations have not yet reached.

Again, this is not a criticism.

It is an observation about implementation context.

---

## Where I Think Her Advice Works Best

I would strongly recommend Elena Verna's work to organisations that already have:

- established experimentation processes,
- access to modern AI tooling,
- technical marketing capability,
- and a culture that supports rapid iteration.

In those environments, her recommendations are both practical and forward-looking.

---

## Where I Would Be More Careful

If a company is still developing its marketing foundations, I would avoid implementing AI-native workflows simply because they appear innovative.

Throughout this repository, I intentionally prioritised recommendations that remained effective even if advanced AI workflows were unavailable.

That decision was based on transferability rather than technological preference.

---

## My Recommendation

I would absolutely recommend reading Elena Verna.

I would not recommend assuming that every workflow she describes can be implemented successfully without first evaluating whether the organisation has the capability to support it.

For me, that distinction became one of the most important lessons from this research.

# What This Exercise Changed For Me

When I started this project, I assumed the value of research came from identifying the "best" experts.

After comparing the ten practitioners included in this repository, I no longer think that is the right objective.

The quality of an expert's advice cannot be separated from the conditions under which that advice was developed.

Throughout this repository, I found myself asking the same question repeatedly:

> "If I removed the assumptions behind this recommendation, would I still make the same decision?"

That question became more useful than asking whether I agreed with the recommendation itself.

It changed the way I evaluated every source.

Instead of comparing experts against each other, I began comparing the assumptions behind their recommendations.

That approach influenced every major decision documented throughout this repository.

---

# What I Would Recommend Instead

If someone asked me today:

> "Which one of these ten practitioners should I follow?"

I would not answer with a single name.

Instead, I would ask a different question.

> "What kind of organisation are you trying to build?"

The answer should determine which recommendations deserve the greatest attention.

For example:

- A company building a long-term content strategy may learn more from Dave Gerhardt, Emily Kramer or Ross Simmonds.
- A company focused on GTM execution and commercial operations may benefit more from Adam Robinson.
- A marketing team investing heavily in AI-enabled workflows may gain more value from Elena Verna.
- A founder-led company trying to build executive visibility may find Kait Stephens or Devin Reed particularly relevant.

None of those recommendations are universally correct.

They become useful when the business context matches the assumptions behind them.

That is the biggest lesson I took away from this research.

---

# My Final Answer to Alex's Question

If I must answer the question directly:

> **Who would I not recommend following?**

My answer is:

**I would not recommend following any practitioner without first understanding the assumptions behind their advice.**

However, if I had to identify one practitioner whose published recommendations I would be least likely to apply unchanged when building the operating model documented in this repository, my answer would be **Adam Robinson**.

That conclusion should not be interpreted as a judgement on his expertise.

It reflects the specific objective of this repository.

His work strengthened my thinking around execution, measurement and operational discipline.

The reason I adapted many of his recommendations is that they often assume organisational maturity that I deliberately chose not to assume while developing this playbook.

If those organisational conditions existed, I would expect my recommendation to change.

For me, that is what good judgement looks like.

Not deciding whether an expert is "right" or "wrong."

Deciding whether the conditions that make their advice effective also exist in the problem I am trying to solve.

# Closing Reflection

This document was more difficult to write than I expected.

It required me to evaluate people whose work I genuinely respect.

That experience reinforced one lesson that influenced the entire repository.

Respecting an expert does not require adopting every recommendation they make.

Likewise, deciding not to apply a recommendation is not the same as dismissing the expertise behind it.

Throughout this repository, I tried to make that distinction explicit.

Whenever I accepted a recommendation, I explained why.

Whenever I rejected or adapted one, I explained the assumptions behind that decision.

Whenever I introduced my own ideas, I labelled them as hypotheses rather than established practices.

If there is one principle that connects every document in this repository, it is this:

**Good research is not about collecting the largest number of recommendations.**

**It is about making transparent, evidence-informed decisions about which recommendations belong together within a single operating model.**

That is the standard I tried to apply throughout this project.

# Final Reflection

Looking back across the entire repository, I think this document changed my understanding of research more than any other.

At the beginning of the project, I assumed that good research meant identifying the best experts and collecting their recommendations.

By the end of the project, I realised that good research requires something different.

It requires deciding **when not to apply a recommendation**, even when it comes from someone whose work you respect.

That was the most difficult judgement I had to make.

It also became the most valuable lesson I learned.

If someone asked me what I would take away from this project, it would not be a single framework or content strategy.

It would be this principle:

> **Good judgement is not demonstrated by agreeing with experts. It is demonstrated by understanding the assumptions behind their advice and deciding whether those assumptions match the problem you are trying to solve.**

That principle influenced every decision documented throughout this repository.

I expect my conclusions to evolve as I continue learning, testing ideas and reviewing new evidence.

What I hope remains consistent is the way I reached those conclusions: by comparing evidence carefully, documenting my reasoning transparently and remaining willing to change my mind when stronger evidence becomes available.
