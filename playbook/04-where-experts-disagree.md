# 05 – Where Experts Disagree

> ## Why this document exists
>
> One of the biggest mistakes I wanted to avoid during this project was treating expert opinions as universal truths.
>
> Although many practitioners agreed on core marketing principles, they often disagreed on implementation.
>
> Rather than ignoring those disagreements, I deliberately documented them.
>
> This document explains:
>
> - where experts disagreed,
> - why they disagreed,
> - how I evaluated the evidence,
> - and which position I ultimately adopted.
>
> I believe these decisions demonstrate more judgment than simply selecting whichever opinion appeared most popular.

---

## Disagreement 01 — Publishing Frequency: Volume vs Quality

### Why This Disagreement Matters

Publishing frequency is one of the easiest areas to turn into a simplistic rule.

More publishing can create more opportunities for discovery, experimentation and audience learning.

However, increasing volume can also become counterproductive if it reduces originality, relevance or quality.

The question I therefore wanted to evaluate was not simply whether companies should publish more or less.

It was whether publishing frequency should be treated as a primary strategic objective or as a variable that should support a broader content strategy.

---

## Contextual Alternative — Frequent Publishing as a General Creator-Economy Norm

### Evidence Status

I could not trace this position to one specific practitioner among the 10 experts researched for this repository.

The recommendation appeared repeatedly as a broader norm within LinkedIn growth and creator-focused content:

- publish frequently,
- build audience familiarity,
- increase opportunities for discovery,
- improve through repetition.

Because I could not attribute this position to a specific practitioner in my collected research, I am not treating it as evidence from one of the ten selected experts.

The underlying assumption is that increased publishing frequency creates more opportunities for audience growth.

I considered this approach because greater volume can be useful when audience growth, experimentation and learning are the primary objectives.

However, I did not want to present a general creator-economy norm as though one of the ten practitioners explicitly made the recommendation.

---

## Position B — Quality and Differentiation

Several practitioners in the research emphasise originality, differentiation, customer understanding, educational value and trust rather than treating content volume as the primary objective.

**Udi Ledergor**

Udi's work emphasises creating content that people genuinely want to consume rather than increasing output simply for the sake of publishing more.

His approach also places importance on educational content, original research and long-term trust.

**Dave Gerhardt**

Dave Gerhardt's research similarly emphasises differentiated writing and developing a recognisable point of view, particularly in an environment where AI makes generic content easier to produce.

The implication I took from this evidence is that publishing more frequently does not automatically make the content more strategically valuable.

---

## My Evaluation

I do not think publishing frequency and content quality are mutually exclusive.

A sustainable cadence is necessary because publishing creates opportunities to learn what resonates with the audience.

However, frequency becomes a weak strategic objective when it is pursued independently of relevance, differentiation and business purpose.

The evidence I found was stronger for treating publishing as part of a learning and distribution system than for treating volume itself as the goal.

That distinction matters because a team can increase the number of posts it publishes without improving the quality of its ideas, its understanding of the audience or its commercial relevance.

---

## My Decision

I choose **quality-first with a sustainable cadence**.

Publishing frequency should be high enough to create learning and distribution opportunities, but it should not become the primary KPI.

I would rather publish fewer differentiated pieces than increase volume by producing repetitive or generic content.

This decision is based on the transferability of the evidence to B2B SaaS rather than a belief that high-frequency publishing is universally ineffective.

The appropriate cadence should therefore be determined by:

- the organisation's ability to maintain quality;
- the amount of original insight available;
- audience response and learning;
- available production capacity;
- and the commercial objective of the content.

---

## Trade-Off

A quality-first approach has a clear downside.

Publishing less frequently can reduce the number of opportunities available for experimentation and discovery.

I therefore would not interpret this decision as a recommendation to publish rarely.

The operating model should maintain a consistent enough cadence to generate learning while protecting the quality and differentiation of each piece.

The trade-off is intentional:

> **Enough volume to learn, enough quality to matter.**

---

# Disagreement 02 — Should AI Generate Content or Assist Content?

## Why this disagreement matters

AI has changed marketing workflows faster than almost any previous technology.

The question is no longer whether AI should be used.

The question is **where AI should stop.**

That boundary significantly influences originality, trust and brand differentiation.

---

## Contextual Alternative — AI-First Content Automation

### Position A — AI Should Generate Most Marketing Content

### Evidence Considered

An AI-first operating model would push content production substantially further toward automation.

Under this model, AI could be used for:

- research,
- idea generation,
- drafting,
- repurposing,
- formatting,
- scheduling,
- publishing,
- and performance analysis.

The underlying assumption is that modern AI can reduce the marginal cost of content production enough that marketing teams should optimise for speed, experimentation and output volume.

The strongest argument for this approach is operational rather than strategic: if AI can produce acceptable content at a fraction of the time and cost of human production, increasing automation creates more opportunities to test topics, formats and messages.

However, I did not identify a sufficiently specific source among the ten practitioners researched for this repository that explicitly recommends allowing AI to generate and publish most external marketing content with minimal human intervention.

I therefore treat this as a **contextual alternative that I considered**, rather than attributing it to one of the ten practitioners.

This distinction is important because I do not want to manufacture an expert disagreement where the research does not support one.

## My Evaluation

T## My Evaluation

The research supports a clear distinction between **automation of execution** and **automation of strategic judgment**.

The practitioners I studied consistently saw substantial value in using AI for research, drafting, analysis, workflow automation and content operations.

However, the evidence I collected did not support handing complete strategic ownership of externally published thought leadership to AI.

That distinction is important.

The question is therefore not whether AI should generate content.

It clearly can.

The question is whether the organisation should allow AI-generated content to bypass human ownership of positioning, customer understanding, differentiation and editorial judgment.

Based on the evidence I reviewed, I do not believe it should.
---


### My Decision

I would not choose one model exclusively.

For B2B SaaS, I would use founder and employee voices as the distribution layer while maintaining a strong company-level knowledge base.

The company should own the underlying ideas, research, customer education and strategic narrative. Individuals should make those ideas human, credible and distributable.

This creates a system in which the company builds the intellectual asset while people create the relationship around it.

**Sources:** Kieran Flanagan, *The New Rules of Marketing*, LinkedIn, 03 July 2026; Udi Ledergor, *Gong's Executive Content & LinkedIn Thought Leadership Playbook*, YouTube, 23 October 2025.
---

## Trade-offs

This approach sacrifices some production speed.

However, it substantially reduces the likelihood of producing generic content that competitors can easily replicate.

---

## When I Would Choose the Alternative

For internal documentation or low-risk operational content, I would permit greater AI autonomy.

For externally published thought leadership, I would continue requiring human ownership.

---

## Final Recommendation

AI should improve execution efficiency.

It should not replace strategic judgment.

# Disagreement 03 — Should LinkedIn Primarily Build Personal Brands or Company Brands?

## Why this disagreement matters

One of the strongest disagreements throughout the research concerned where companies should invest their organic marketing efforts.

Some practitioners believe founders and executives should become the primary distribution channel.

Others argue that the company brand itself should become the trusted authority.

The answer affects hiring decisions, content ownership, distribution strategy and long-term marketing resilience.

---

## Position A — Founder-Led Brands Build Trust Faster

### Evidence Considered

**Dave Gerhardt**

**Title:** Timeless B2B Marketing Lessons

**Source:** Exit Five Newsletter

**Date:** 23 Jun 2026

https://exitfive.com/newsletter/timeless-marketing-lessons/

Dave consistently argues that people naturally trust people before companies. Founder-led content creates familiarity and credibility that traditional corporate pages rarely achieve.

---

**Devin Reed**

**Title:** Building an Executive Brand on LinkedIn

**Source:** YouTube

**Date:** 29 Jul 2025

https://youtu.be/mu72K4ezJk0

Devin demonstrates how executive branding strengthens trust because audiences connect with authentic expertise and lived experience rather than institutional messaging.

Position A is not represented by any of my 10 researched experts; it is the dominant creator-economy advice observed across linkedin growth content.
---

## Position B — Company Brands Create More Durable Marketing Assets

### Evidence Considered

**Udi Ledergor**

**Title:** Most B2B Marketing Doesn't Fail Because It's Bad

**Source:** LinkedIn

**Date:** 27 Feb 2026

https://www.linkedin.com/posts/udiledergor_most-b2b-marketing-doesnt-fail-because-its-activity-7433173432332267522-WO2k

Udi argues that companies ultimately need brands capable of standing independently of individual employees.

Strong category positioning should survive leadership changes.

---

## My Evaluation

I do not believe this is an either-or decision.

Founder brands create trust more quickly.

Company brands preserve trust over longer time horizons.

Rather than choosing one, I evaluated which sequence creates stronger long-term outcomes.

---

## My Decision

I chose a **Founder-Led → Company-Led** progression.

In the early stages, founders should become the primary educators because people naturally engage with individuals.

As brand awareness grows, the company should gradually become the central knowledge hub while founders continue reinforcing credibility.

This approach captures the strengths of both positions.

---

## Trade-offs

Advantages

- Faster trust development.
- Higher engagement.
- Stronger storytelling.
- Better executive credibility.

Disadvantages

- Increased dependence on founders.
- More difficult scaling.
- Potential brand disruption if leadership changes.

---

## When I Would Choose the Alternative

If the organisation already possesses strong brand recognition or operates in a highly regulated industry where institutional trust outweighs personal visibility, I would invest more heavily in company-led content.

---

## Final Recommendation

Treat founder branding as the accelerator.

Treat company branding as the destination.

# Disagreement 04 — Should Marketing Optimise for Demand Capture or Demand Creation?

## Why this disagreement matters

This disagreement influences nearly every content decision.

Some marketers prioritise capturing existing buying intent.

Others invest in educating future buyers before purchasing intent exists.

The choice determines both content strategy and success metrics.

---

Adam focuses on building repeatable GTM systems that create measurable commercial outcomes through disciplined execution.

Position A is not represented by any of my 10 researched experts; it is the dominant creator-economy advice observed across linkedin growth content.
---

## Position B — Create Future Demand Through Education

### Evidence Considered

**Dave Gerhardt**

**Title:** Timeless B2B Marketing Lessons

**Source:** Exit Five Newsletter

**Date:** 23 Jun 2026

https://exitfive.com/newsletter/timeless-marketing-lessons/

---

**Udi Ledergor**

**Title:** Most B2B Marketing Doesn't Fail Because It's Bad

**Source:** LinkedIn

**Date:** 27 Feb 2026

https://www.linkedin.com/posts/udiledergor_most-b2b-marketing-doesnt-fail-because-its-activity-7433173432332267522-WO2k

Both practitioners repeatedly emphasise educating the market before buyers actively enter purchasing cycles.

---

## My Evaluation

The disagreement initially appeared larger than it actually is.

Demand capture and demand creation solve different business problems.

Demand capture converts buyers who already intend to purchase.

Demand creation increases the number of future buyers.

Neither replaces the other.

---

## My Decision

I chose to optimise LinkedIn Organic Content primarily for **demand creation**.

LinkedIn provides a unique opportunity to educate prospective customers long before they begin evaluating vendors.

Demand capture should remain the responsibility of other marketing channels such as product pages, search and sales processes.

---

## Trade-offs

Advantages

- Stronger long-term brand equity.
- Greater customer trust.
- Better positioning.
- Sustainable audience growth.

Disadvantages

- Longer feedback cycles.
- Less immediate attribution.
- Greater executive patience required.

---

## When I Would Choose the Alternative

For companies with immediate revenue constraints, I would temporarily increase investment in demand capture while continuing to allocate resources toward long-term demand creation.

The balance should change as business maturity evolves.

---

## Final Recommendation

Use LinkedIn primarily to create future demand.

Use transactional channels to capture existing demand.

Those objectives complement each other rather than compete.

# Disagreement 05 — Should Content Prioritise Broad Reach or a Narrow ICP?

## Why this disagreement matters

One of the most common pieces of marketing advice is to "reach more people."

However, multiple practitioners questioned whether maximising audience size should actually be the objective.

The disagreement is fundamentally about efficiency versus relevance.

Should content attempt to attract the largest possible audience, or intentionally resonate with a much smaller group of ideal buyers?

This decision influences messaging, positioning, distribution and long-term brand perception.

---

## Position A — Broader Reach Creates More Opportunities

### Evidence Considered

Many creator-led marketing strategies encourage broad positioning because larger audiences generally produce:

- higher engagement,
- faster follower growth,
- increased visibility,
- greater algorithmic reach.

This philosophy assumes that opportunity increases proportionally with audience size.

Position A is not represented by any of my 10 researched experts; it is the dominant creator-economy advice observed across linkedin growth content.
---

## Position B — Narrow Relevance Creates Better Commercial Outcomes

### Evidence Considered

**Kyle Poyar**

**Title:** The Real Marketing Problems Companies Need to Solve

**Source:** LinkedIn

**Date:** 08 May 2026

https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kyle-poyar_why-we-think-we-have-a-marketing-problem-activity-7458494561871593472--Uza

Kyle consistently argues that marketing becomes significantly more effective when it solves clearly defined customer problems rather than attempting to appeal to everyone.

---

**Dave Gerhardt**

**Title:** Timeless B2B Marketing Lessons

**Source:** Exit Five Newsletter

**Date:** 23 Jun 2026

https://exitfive.com/newsletter/timeless-marketing-lessons/

Dave repeatedly encourages marketers to become indispensable to a specific audience before attempting broader expansion.

---

## My Evaluation

Initially, broad reach appears attractive because visibility is easy to measure.

However, throughout the research I noticed that practitioners measuring business outcomes consistently prioritised audience relevance over audience size.

A post viewed by 5,000 ideal buyers is usually more valuable than one viewed by 100,000 people outside the target market.

That distinction significantly changed how I evaluated content performance.

---

## My Decision

I deliberately chose to optimise for **ICP relevance before audience scale**.

Growth remains important.

However, I believe growth should result from repeatedly serving the right audience rather than continuously broadening the message.

---

## Trade-offs

Advantages

- Stronger positioning.
- Higher trust.
- Better sales conversations.
- Greater customer resonance.

Disadvantages

- Slower follower growth.
- Lower viral potential.
- Smaller addressable audience.

---

## When I Would Choose the Alternative

If my objective were recruiting, employer branding or creator monetisation, broader audience reach would become significantly more important.

For B2B SaaS demand generation, I believe ICP relevance remains the stronger operating decision.

---

## Final Recommendation

Measure influence within the right audience rather than popularity across the entire platform.

# Disagreement 06 — Should Success Be Measured by Engagement Metrics or Business Outcomes?

## Why this disagreement matters

Every marketing team measures performance.

The more difficult question is deciding what should actually be measured.

Throughout the research I found substantial disagreement regarding which metrics deserve the greatest attention.

Some practitioners emphasise engagement.

Others prioritise business impact.

The chosen metrics inevitably shape marketing behaviour.

---

## Position A — Engagement Indicates Content Success

### Evidence Considered

Many social media frameworks evaluate success primarily through:

- impressions,
- likes,
- comments,
- shares,
- follower growth.

These metrics provide immediate feedback and are easy to monitor over time.

## Position B — Business Outcomes Matter More Than Platform Metrics

### Evidence Considered

**Adam Robinson**

**Title:** Going $0–1M ARR (Again): My 5-Step Plan for Creating a Lean, Mean, High-Velocity Growth Machine

**Source:** Newsletter

**Date:** 11 Jun 2026

https://newsletter.rb2b.com/p/going-0-1m-arr-again

Adam consistently evaluates marketing based on commercial outcomes rather than platform activity.

---

**Emily Kramer**

**Title:** I Analyzed 100 B2B Marketing Teams with Claude Code

**Source:** Newsletter

**Date:** 21 Apr 2026

https://newsletter.mkt1.co/p/state-of-marketing-report-teams-part-1

Emily repeatedly encourages marketers to connect measurement directly to strategic decision-making instead of isolated campaign metrics.

---

## My Evaluation

Engagement metrics are useful.

However, they should be treated as diagnostic indicators rather than primary objectives.

High engagement does not necessarily indicate stronger positioning, increased trust or improved commercial performance.

Conversely, highly valuable content may receive modest engagement while significantly influencing customer conversations and buying decisions.

This distinction became one of the most important conclusions of my research.

---

## My Decision

I decided to organise measurement into three levels.

### Level 1 — Business Outcomes

Primary indicators.

Examples:

- qualified pipeline,
- customer conversations,
- inbound opportunities,
- sales influence.

---

### Level 2 — Learning Metrics

Indicators that improve future decision-making.

Examples:

- recurring customer questions,
- objections,
- content requests,
- qualitative feedback.

---

### Level 3 — Platform Metrics

Supporting indicators.

Examples:

- impressions,
- engagement,
- follower growth,
- click-through rate.

These remain valuable but should not become the primary definition of success.

---

## Trade-offs

Advantages

- Better strategic alignment.
- Reduced focus on vanity metrics.
- Improved long-term optimisation.
- More meaningful performance discussions.

Disadvantages

- Harder attribution.
- Longer evaluation cycles.
- Greater dependence on cross-functional data.

---

## When I Would Choose the Alternative

If I were responsible solely for social media performance reporting, engagement metrics would naturally receive greater emphasis.

However, when responsible for business growth, I believe commercial outcomes deserve priority.

---

## Final Recommendation

Measure engagement.

Learn from engagement.

But optimise for business outcomes.

# Closing Reflection

When I started this research, I expected to identify a single "best" approach to LinkedIn Organic Content.

Instead, I found something more valuable.

Experienced practitioners often agree on principles while disagreeing on implementation.

That observation fundamentally changed how I approached this playbook.

Rather than searching for perfect answers, I focused on understanding:

- why intelligent practitioners reached different conclusions,
- which assumptions influenced their recommendations,
- and under what conditions each approach becomes appropriate.

This document therefore represents my decision-making process rather than a ranking of experts.

Every recommendation I adopted involved trade-offs.

Every recommendation I rejected still contained valuable ideas.

The objective was never to prove one practitioner correct and another incorrect.

The objective was to build an operating model that remains defensible, transparent and adaptable as new evidence emerges.

For me, that is the real value of practitioner research.

Not finding certainty.

Learning how to make better decisions when certainty does not exist.
