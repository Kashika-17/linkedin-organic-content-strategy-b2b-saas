# Limitation 01 — The Evidence Base Is Practitioner-Led Rather Than Experimental

## Observation

Most of the evidence used throughout this repository comes from experienced B2B SaaS practitioners.

Examples include newsletters, podcasts, LinkedIn posts, interviews and public presentations.

These sources provide practical implementation experience rather than controlled experimental research.

---

## Why This Matters

Practitioner experience is valuable because it reflects real business environments.

However, experience is also shaped by context.

Company size, product maturity, market conditions, team structure and customer profile all influence whether a recommendation succeeds.

That means I cannot assume every recommendation transfers equally well to every organisation.

---

## How I Addressed This Limitation

Rather than relying on a single expert, I compared recommendations across all ten practitioners included in this repository.

Whenever similar ideas appeared independently across multiple sources, I treated those recommendations with greater confidence.

Where recommendations conflicted, I documented the disagreement instead of forcing a single "correct" answer.

This approach improved confidence but did not eliminate uncertainty.

---

## Remaining Risk

Some recommendations included in this operating model may perform differently in organisations that differ substantially from those represented in the research.

Examples might include:

- early-stage startups,
- enterprise organisations,
- non-SaaS businesses,
- heavily regulated industries.

I therefore consider this repository more transferable to modern B2B SaaS companies than to every possible marketing environment.

---

## My Conclusion

Based on the evidence I reviewed, I believe practitioner consensus provides a practical foundation for decision-making.

However, implementation should always be followed by experimentation rather than blind adoption.

# Limitation 02 — The Repository Reflects Publicly Available Knowledge

## Observation

The research for this project relied on publicly available material published by recognised practitioners.

That material naturally emphasises recommendations they are willing to share publicly.

It does not provide complete visibility into:

- internal decision-making,
- unsuccessful experiments,
- abandoned strategies,
- confidential company data.

---

## Why This Matters

Public content often explains what worked.

It explains unsuccessful experiments far less frequently.

As a result, the available evidence is likely affected by survivorship bias.

Some recommendations may appear stronger simply because unsuccessful implementations are rarely documented.

---

## How I Addressed This Limitation

Rather than accepting recommendations individually, I compared:

- recurring themes,
- conflicting viewpoints,
- implementation context,
- and stated assumptions.

I also separated evidence from my own interpretation throughout the repository so readers can distinguish practitioner recommendations from my conclusions.

---

## Remaining Risk

There may be valuable lessons that are absent from the available evidence simply because they have not been published.

This limitation cannot be completely removed without access to internal company research.

---

## My Conclusion

This repository should be interpreted as a synthesis of publicly available practitioner knowledge rather than a complete representation of everything successful B2B marketing teams know.

# Untested Assumption 01 — Educational Content Will Produce Stronger Long-Term Business Outcomes

## Observation

Throughout the sources I reviewed, educational content appeared far more frequently than promotional content.

Across several practitioners in this repository—including Dave Gerhardt, Emily Kramer, Ross Simmonds and Udi Ledergor—I found recurring support for an education-first approach.

After comparing those recurring recommendations, I chose to make education-first one of the core principles of this operating model.

---

## Why I Consider This an Assumption

Although the recommendation appeared repeatedly across my research, I did not have access to internal performance data from these companies.

I therefore cannot independently verify that educational content consistently outperforms more product-led approaches across different industries, company sizes or market conditions.

The available evidence demonstrates that experienced practitioners believe this approach works.

It does not prove that it is universally optimal.

---

## What I Would Test

If I were implementing this playbook inside a company, I would compare three editorial approaches over one quarter.

**Approach A**

Education-first content.

**Approach B**

Balanced educational and product-led content.

**Approach C**

Primarily product-focused content.

Rather than evaluating only engagement metrics, I would compare:

- qualified inbound conversations,
- pipeline influence,
- customer trust signals,
- sales feedback,
- and repeat audience engagement.

---

## My Conclusion

Based on the evidence reviewed, I believe an education-first strategy is the most defensible starting point.

However, I would treat this as an operational hypothesis that should be validated using company-specific data rather than accepted as a universal rule.

# Untested Assumption 02 — Human Judgment Will Continue to Differentiate Marketing

## Observation

A recurring conclusion across the sources included in this repository was that AI improves execution while humans remain responsible for customer understanding, positioning and strategic judgment.

That observation directly influenced several recommendations throughout this playbook.

---

## Why I Consider This an Assumption

AI capabilities are evolving rapidly.

The recommendations available at the time of this research reflect current workflows rather than future capabilities.

It is entirely possible that AI will eventually perform some strategic tasks more effectively than practitioners currently expect.

I do not believe the available evidence is strong enough to predict where that boundary will exist in the future.

---

## What I Would Test

Rather than debating this theoretically, I would review AI workflows every six months.

For each marketing activity, I would ask:

- Does AI improve quality?
- Does AI reduce originality?
- Does AI improve customer understanding?
- Does AI increase or decrease trust?
- Does human review still add measurable value?

The answers to these questions should determine how responsibilities evolve.

---

However, I also believe this recommendation should be revisited as better evidence becomes available rather than treated as permanently correct.

# What Is Missing From This Repository?

While reviewing the completed repository, I identified several areas that I believe remain underexplored.

These gaps do not invalidate the operating model, but they represent opportunities for future research.

## 1. Internal Company Data

This repository relies on publicly available practitioner knowledge.

I did not have access to:

- CRM data,
- attribution reports,
- pipeline metrics,
- customer retention analysis,
- or internal experimentation results.

Those sources would likely strengthen or challenge several recommendations documented here.

---

## 2. Customer Interviews

The repository evaluates practitioner recommendations rather than direct customer perspectives.

If this operating model were implemented within a real organisation, I would validate the recommendations through structured customer interviews before treating them as established practice.

---

## 3. Longitudinal Testing

Most recommendations were evaluated by comparing practitioner perspectives at a single point in time.

I was not able to observe how these recommendations performed over multiple years or changing market conditions.

For that reason, I believe the operating model should be reviewed periodically rather than treated as static documentation.

# Reflection

One of the most valuable lessons from this project was recognising the difference between **confidence** and **certainty**.

Confidence comes from comparing evidence, identifying recurring patterns and making decisions despite incomplete information.

Certainty would require evidence that simply was not available to me.

For that reason, I intentionally avoided presenting this repository as a definitive guide to LinkedIn Organic Content.

Instead, I view it as an evidence-informed operating model that should continue evolving as new customer insights, experiments and practitioner research become available.

I believe acknowledging those limitations strengthens the quality of the work rather than weakening it because it makes the reasoning behind each decision transparent.

# What Would Change My Mind?

Throughout this repository, I made a series of strategic decisions based on the evidence I reviewed.

Those decisions should not be interpreted as permanent conclusions.

They reflect the evidence available to me at the time of this project.

If stronger evidence became available, I would expect parts of this operating model to change.

Rather than viewing that as a weakness, I believe it is an essential part of evidence-informed decision-making.

Below are the situations that would lead me to reconsider the recommendations documented throughout this repository.

---

## 1. Repeated Customer Evidence That Contradicts the Current Operating Model

One of the biggest influences on my recommendations was the recurring advice that appeared across multiple practitioners included in this repository.

However, if direct customer research consistently contradicted those recommendations, I would prioritise customer evidence.

For example, if multiple customer interviews demonstrated that educational content was not improving trust or influencing buying decisions for a particular audience, I would revisit the education-first approach documented throughout this playbook.

For me, customer behaviour should outweigh practitioner opinion when the two are in conflict.

---

## 2. Internal Company Experiments Produce Better Results

Several recommendations in this repository should be viewed as starting points rather than fixed operating rules.

If controlled internal experiments repeatedly demonstrated that an alternative workflow produced stronger business outcomes, I would adopt the evidence from those experiments.

For example, if a company consistently observed that a more product-led content strategy generated better qualified pipeline without reducing customer trust, I would reconsider the balance between educational and promotional content.

The purpose of this playbook is to support better decisions, not defend previous ones.

---

## 3. Practitioner Consensus Changes

Throughout the research for this repository...

If future research showed that several independent practitioners reached different conclusions based on new evidence or changing market conditions, I would review the assumptions documented throughout this repository.

I believe practitioner consensus should inform decisions, but it should also be questioned as industries evolve.

---

## 4. AI Changes the Nature of Marketing Work

One of the central assumptions in this repository is that AI strengthens execution while humans remain responsible for strategic judgement.

I reached that conclusion after reviewing the available sources.

However, I do not assume that this boundary will remain constant.

If future evidence demonstrated that AI could reliably contribute to positioning, customer insight or editorial judgement without reducing originality or trust, I would update the operating model accordingly.

My objective is not to preserve today's workflow.

It is to preserve the quality of decision-making as technology changes.

# Final Reflection

At the beginning of this project, I expected the outcome to be a collection of best practices.

Instead, I finished with something different.

I finished with a decision-making framework.

The most valuable lesson was not identifying which expert was "right."

It was learning how to compare evidence, recognise uncertainty and make decisions that remain open to revision.

That perspective influenced every document in this repository.

Whenever the available evidence was strong, I explained why I accepted it.

Whenever experts disagreed, I compared their reasoning before making a decision.

Whenever I introduced my own ideas, I described them as hypotheses rather than established practices.

Whenever I identified weaknesses, I documented the assumptions that still require further validation.

I intentionally structured the repository this way because I believe good strategy is not defined by certainty.

It is defined by transparent reasoning.

If I revisit this repository a year from now, I do not expect every recommendation to remain unchanged.

I hope some of them change.

That would mean new evidence became available and the operating model improved as a result.

For me, that is the purpose of research.

Not to produce conclusions that never change, but to build a decision-making process that continues improving as better evidence emerges.

# Appendix — Personal Research Principles

While completing this project, I found myself returning to the same principles whenever I had to choose between conflicting recommendations.

Rather than leaving those principles implicit, I decided to document them here because they influenced every decision made throughout this repository.

These are not rules I found in a newsletter or podcast.

They are the principles I used while evaluating the evidence.

## 1. Agreement Is Not Evidence

If several practitioners repeated the same recommendation, I treated that as a reason to investigate further—not as proof that the recommendation was correct.

Where possible, I looked for the assumptions behind the advice before deciding whether it belonged in this operating model.

---

## 2. Context Matters More Than Popularity

Several recommendations appeared persuasive until I considered the context in which they were originally shared.

Before adopting any recommendation, I asked:

> "Would I still make this decision if the company, audience or business objective were different?"

If the answer was no, I treated the recommendation as context-dependent rather than universally applicable.

---

## 3. Public Advice Is Not the Same as Complete Knowledge

Every practitioner included in this repository shares ideas publicly.

That does not necessarily mean those ideas represent everything they know or everything they implement internally.

For that reason, I treated public content as one source of evidence rather than a complete picture of modern B2B marketing.

---

## 4. My Decisions Should Be Easier to Challenge Than My Conclusions

Throughout this repository, I tried to explain *why* I reached each decision rather than simply presenting the decision itself.

My expectation is not that every reader will agree with every recommendation.

Instead, I wanted readers to understand the reasoning well enough that they could challenge it if better evidence became available.

I believe that produces stronger discussions than asking people to accept conclusions without understanding the thinking behind them.

---

## Closing Note

When I started this project, I thought the challenge would be finding the right answers.

Instead, I realised the more difficult challenge was asking better questions.

Comparing experts was valuable.

Deciding when **not** to follow them was more valuable.

Creating my own hypotheses was valuable.

Being clear that they are **hypotheses rather than facts** was even more valuable.

If there is one idea that I hope carries through the entire repository, it is this:

> Good marketing decisions are not built on confidence alone.

> They are built on transparent reasoning, evidence that can be challenged and a willingness to change when better evidence becomes available.
