# 06 – What I Rejected and Why

> # Why this document exists
>
> > # Why this document exists
>
> One of the objectives of this project was to demonstrate independent judgement rather than simply collect practitioner recommendations.
>
> Throughout the research process, I encountered many ideas that initially appeared persuasive.
>
> Some were widely accepted within B2B marketing.
>
> Others came from practitioners whose work I respect and referenced elsewhere in this repository.
>
> However, after comparing evidence across multiple sources, evaluating implementation assumptions and considering the specific objectives of this operating model, I deliberately chose not to adopt every recommendation.
>
> Rejecting a recommendation should not be interpreted as rejecting the expertise behind it.
>
> Instead, it reflects a judgement that, within the context of this repository, another approach was either:
>
> - more transferable,
> - better supported by recurring evidence,
> - more consistent with the operating principles documented earlier,
> - or better aligned with the objectives of a LinkedIn Organic Content strategy for B2B SaaS.
>
> I believe documenting these decisions demonstrates judgement more effectively than presenting only the recommendations I accepted.

# Rejected Recommendation 01 — "Post Every Day"

## Why it initially seemed convincing

This was one of the most common recommendations I encountered while researching LinkedIn content.

The reasoning appears intuitive.

Publishing more frequently creates:

- more impressions,
- more engagement opportunities,
- faster writing improvement,
- greater algorithm visibility.

For creator businesses, these outcomes can be highly valuable.

Initially, this recommendation appeared reasonable.

---

## Why I Rejected It

After comparing the evidence, I found little support for treating publishing frequency as a strategic objective.

Most experienced practitioners consistently discussed:

- originality,
- customer understanding,
- positioning,
- trust.

Daily publishing was usually presented as an execution tactic rather than a business principle.

I therefore concluded that publishing frequency should remain flexible.

Quality should remain non-negotiable.

---

## What I Chose Instead

Rather than defining success as:

> "Publish every day."

I replaced it with:

> "Publish consistently when the content strengthens customer trust."

This shifts attention away from activity and towards business value.

---

## Trade-offs

Rejecting daily publishing means:

- slower audience growth,
- fewer algorithm opportunities,
- lower content volume.

However, it also creates:

- stronger positioning,
- higher editorial standards,
- greater differentiation,
- reduced AI-generated repetition.

---

## When I Would Use It

If I were building a creator business where audience size directly determined revenue, I would significantly increase publishing frequency.

For B2B SaaS brand building, I believe quality-first remains the stronger operating decision.

## Conditions Under Which I Would Reconsider

This recommendation is not permanently rejected.

I would reconsider it if future evidence demonstrated that the underlying assumptions had changed or if the operating context differed materially from the one considered throughout this repository.

Until then, I believe the alternative documented above remains the stronger operating decision.
---

## Final Decision

Daily publishing is a useful execution tactic.

It should not become the primary objective of a LinkedIn Organic Content strategy.

# Rejected Recommendation 02 — "AI Should Produce Complete First Drafts"

Modern AI tools dramatically reduce writing time.

Allowing AI to generate complete first drafts appears attractive because it:

## Why I Rejected It

Although every practitioner encouraged using AI, none suggested replacing strategic thinking.

Instead, they consistently described AI as a capability that amplifies experienced marketers.

The strongest competitive advantage remained:

- customer understanding,
- positioning,
- differentiated thinking,
- editorial judgment.

Those capabilities remain fundamentally human.

---

## What I Chose Instead

Within this operating model:

Humans own:

- positioning,
- messaging,
- customer insight,
- editorial approval.

AI supports:

- research,
- drafting,
- summarisation,
- repurposing,
- workflow automation.

---

## Trade-offs

This approach sacrifices production speed.

However, it significantly reduces the likelihood of producing generic marketing content that competitors can easily replicate.

---

## When I Would Use It

For internal documentation, process documentation or operational content, I would allow AI to generate complete drafts.

For externally published thought leadership, I would continue requiring human ownership.

## Conditions Under Which I Would Reconsider

This recommendation is not permanently rejected.

I would reconsider it if future evidence demonstrated that the underlying assumptions had changed or if the operating context differed materially from the one considered throughout this repository.

Until then, I believe the alternative documented above remains the stronger operating decision.

---

## Final Decision

AI should improve execution.

It should not replace marketing judgment.

# Rejected Recommendation 03 — "Write for the Algorithm"

## Why it initially seemed convincing

Many LinkedIn growth discussions focus heavily on understanding the platform algorithm.

Common recommendations include:

- posting at specific times,
- using formatting patterns,
- maximising dwell time,
- increasing comments,
- encouraging engagement signals.

At first glance, these recommendations appear practical because they promise greater visibility.

For marketers under pressure to demonstrate results quickly, optimising for the algorithm can feel like the fastest path to growth.

---

## Evidence Considered

### Creator-led LinkedIn advice

A recurring theme across creator communities is that understanding algorithm behaviour improves content performance.

Most recommendations focus on increasing reach rather than improving the quality of thinking.

---

### Dave Gerhardt

**Title:** Timeless B2B Marketing Lessons

**Source:** Exit Five Newsletter

**Date:** 23 Jun 2026

https://exitfive.com/newsletter/timeless-marketing-lessons/

Dave consistently argues that memorable brands are built through trust, relevance and differentiated positioning rather than platform manipulation.

---

### Ross Simmonds

**Title:** Create Once, Distribute Forever

**Source:** YouTube

**Date:** 09 Dec 2024

https://youtu.be/7_bFP2iVVN0

Ross focuses on increasing the lifetime value of quality content through distribution instead of relying on algorithmic optimisation.

---

## Why I Rejected It

I deliberately chose not to optimise this operating model around algorithm behaviour because algorithms constantly evolve.

Customer problems evolve much more slowly.

Building a marketing system around platform mechanics creates operational instability.

Building it around customer needs creates durability.

For that reason, I treated algorithm awareness as useful operational knowledge—not as a strategic principle.

---

## What I Chose Instead

Rather than asking:

> "What will the algorithm reward?"

I replaced the question with:

> "What will our ideal customer genuinely find valuable enough to remember or share?"

That question consistently produces stronger long-term marketing decisions.

---

## Trade-offs

Advantages of my approach:

- More durable strategy.
- Better customer trust.
- Greater brand differentiation.
- Reduced dependence on platform updates.

Disadvantages:

- Slower experimentation with platform-specific opportunities.
- Potentially lower short-term reach.

---

## When I Would Use the Alternative

If LinkedIn introduced a significant product feature that materially changed content discovery, I would absolutely adapt execution tactics.

However, I would avoid redesigning the overall strategy around temporary platform behaviour.


## Conditions Under Which I Would Reconsider

This recommendation is not permanently rejected.

I would reconsider it if future evidence demonstrated that the underlying assumptions had changed or if the operating context differed materially from the one considered throughout this repository.

Until then, I believe the alternative documented above remains the stronger operating decision.
---

## Final Decision

Understand the algorithm.

Do not build the business around it.

# Rejected Recommendation 04 — "Always Talk About Your Product"

## Why it initially seemed convincing

Many B2B companies naturally believe their marketing should consistently highlight product capabilities.

This approach appears commercially logical.

If the objective is increasing product awareness, discussing the product more frequently seems like the obvious solution.

Initially, I also considered whether stronger product promotion should become a core operating principle.

https://www.linkedin.com/posts/devinreed_you-shouldnt-write-like-a-journalist-analyst-activity-7459984518205136896-Cw5c

**Title:** Most B2B Marketing Doesn't Fail Because It's Bad

**Source:** LinkedIn

**Date:** 27 Feb 2026

https://www.linkedin.com/posts/udiledergor_most-b2b-marketing-doesnt-fail-because-its-activity-7433173432332267522-WO2k

Udi repeatedly encourages marketers to educate the market before expecting customers to appreciate product differentiation.

---

## Why I Rejected It

Throughout the research, I noticed a recurring pattern.

The strongest practitioners rarely led with their products.

Instead, they led with:

- customer understanding,
- educational insights,
- industry observations,
- strategic thinking.

Products appeared naturally after credibility had already been established.

This sequence proved significantly more persuasive than leading every conversation with product features.

---

## What I Chose Instead

Rather than making products the centre of every conversation, I positioned products as evidence that supports expertise.

The communication sequence becomes:

Customer problem

↓

Insight

↓

Education

↓

Perspective

↓

Product (where genuinely relevant)

This approach allows the product to reinforce authority rather than interrupt it.

---

## Trade-offs

Advantages:

- Stronger audience trust.
- Better educational value.
- Higher long-term engagement.
- Improved positioning.

Disadvantages:

- Slower product exposure.
- Less direct attribution.
- Requires patience from leadership.

---

## When I Would Use the Alternative

For:

- product launches,
- pricing announcements,
- feature releases,
- customer success stories,

I would intentionally increase product visibility.

Outside those situations, I would continue prioritising educational content.


## Conditions Under Which I Would Reconsider

This recommendation is not permanently rejected.

I would reconsider it if future evidence demonstrated that the underlying assumptions had changed or if the operating context differed materially from the one considered throughout this repository.

Until then, I believe the alternative documented above remains the stronger operating decision.
---

## Final Decision

Products should appear naturally as part of solving customer problems—not become the starting point of every conversation.

# Rejected Recommendation 05 — "Measure Success Primarily Through Engagement Metrics"

## Why it initially seemed convincing

Engagement metrics are the most visible indicators available to marketers.

Impressions, likes, comments and follower growth are easy to track, easy to report and provide immediate feedback after publishing.

Because these metrics are readily available, it is tempting to make them the primary definition of success.

Initially, I considered using engagement as the main optimisation target for this playbook.

---

## Why I Rejected It

I concluded that engagement should remain an input into decision-making rather than the objective itself.

A highly engaged audience is valuable only if that engagement contributes to stronger customer understanding, improved positioning or commercial outcomes.

Optimising purely for engagement creates incentives to produce content that attracts attention rather than content that creates trust.

That trade-off conflicts with the operating principles established earlier in this repository.

---

## What I Chose Instead

I organised measurement into three levels.

1. Business outcomes.
2. Learning outcomes.
3. Platform engagement.

This ensures engagement informs decisions without becoming the primary objective.

---

## Trade-offs

Advantages

- Better strategic alignment.
- Reduced focus on vanity metrics.
- Stronger commercial thinking.

Disadvantages

- Harder measurement.
- Longer evaluation cycles.
- Greater dependence on qualitative customer feedback.

---

## When I Would Use the Alternative

If I were responsible solely for growing a creator account or managing social media reporting, engagement would naturally become a higher priority.

For B2B SaaS growth, I believe it should remain a supporting metric.


## Conditions Under Which I Would Reconsider

This recommendation is not permanently rejected.

I would reconsider it if future evidence demonstrated that the underlying assumptions had changed or if the operating context differed materially from the one considered throughout this repository.

Until then, I believe the alternative documented above remains the stronger operating decision.
---

## Final Decision

Measure engagement.

Learn from engagement.

Do not optimise exclusively for engagement.

# My Rejection Framework

# My Rejection Framework

Before rejecting any recommendation, I required evidence rather than intuition.

The framework below was applied consistently throughout this document to ensure that rejection decisions remained transparent, repeatable and aligned with the research methodology documented in Document 01.

Rather than rejecting recommendations because I personally disagreed with them, I evaluated whether they remained appropriate for the operating model developed throughout this repository.

Every rejected recommendation satisfied at least one of the following criteria.

| Rejection Criterion | Reason |
|---------------------|--------|
| Context-dependent | The recommendation only worked under specific business conditions. |
| Weak supporting evidence | The recommendation appeared infrequently or lacked convincing justification. |
| Conflict with stronger evidence | Multiple independent practitioners consistently supported an alternative approach. |
| Low transferability | The recommendation depended on resources, scale or organisational maturity that many B2B SaaS companies would not possess. |
| Inconsistent with this operating model | The recommendation conflicted with the operating principles established in Documents 01–05. |

This framework helped ensure that rejection decisions were based on evidence and implementation context rather than personal preference.

# What This Taught Me

Before beginning this project, I assumed the value of research came from collecting more recommendations.

I now believe the opposite.

The value of research comes from learning **which recommendations not to follow**.

That shift fundamentally changed how I approached strategy.

Instead of asking,

> "Which expert is right?"

I began asking,

> "Under what conditions is this recommendation useful, and does it fit the operating model I am trying to build?"

That question produced significantly better decisions than simply counting how many experts agreed.

---

# Closing Reflection

I intentionally included this document because I believe strategy is defined as much by what an organisation chooses **not** to do as by what it decides to prioritise.

Every rejected recommendation in this document came from an experienced practitioner or a widely accepted marketing belief.

None were rejected because they lacked merit.

They were rejected because, after comparing the available evidence, evaluating competing viewpoints and considering implementation trade-offs, I concluded that they were less transferable to the operating model developed throughout this repository.

For me, that is the difference between research and judgment.

Research expands the range of possible decisions.

Judgement determines which of those decisions should actually become part of an operating model.

That distinction became one of the most valuable lessons I learned while completing this project.

It is also the principle that connects every document in this repository.
